# A Memory of Pangea

> [!NOTE]  
> These are the memories of a man with a poor memory. Some are hazy; thirty years have gone by. 
> If you have corrections, they are welcome.




- 241229

A long time ago, in a galaxy far, far away....


_Pangea is an initiative that began in July 1996 with the aim of building and running the first true Civic Network of Varese, a real virtual square where the people, associations and institutions that live and work in Varese and its province can meet. Its purpose is to simplify and encourage human relations and collaboration through the intelligent and innovative use of information technology. The goal, in fact, is to improve the quality of social life and to strengthen the sense of belonging to the local community._

_Pangea does not want to be a colorful shop window like the Internet is today, where the user 'surfs' through information pages created by others: Pangea is built mainly from the information, knowledge and experiences that each person chooses to share with the others. Every user is at once the object and an active subject of communication, which in Pangea is two-way and open to everyone's contribution._

_Pangea offers everyone the chance to connect and take part: access is free of charge, registration formalities are minimal, and the program used can be operated even by those who do not know computers. Moreover, a special technical feature of the program (the off-line reader) cuts the length of telephone connections down to just a few minutes, at a cost far lower than that of connecting to the Internet._




## Background

To understand, thirty years later, what Pangea was, we must first take into account the things that are most different today. The first that comes to mind is that we are now used to digital communication that is always on, very cheap, and used by everyone.

### A Couple of Steps into Prehistory


The situation in Italy in the mid-90s was still profoundly different. First of all, the network was still run by SIP (the monopolist until 1996), and the telephone network still worked according to the logic of a monopoly: the impression a user got was that the telephone operator was doing you a favor by installing a device, on its own schedule and for its own fees, and then reselling you its use at the rates it had decided. The telephone network was still almost entirely analog; DTMF dialing was a novelty, and it became available where I lived at the beginning of the 90s.

From the point of view of telematics, there were some important limitations:

- in theory, only approved devices could be connected to the network. You had to get these devices from the monopolist, so in general the modem you had bought abroad was not approved, and probably neither was the one you had bought at the computer shop. As a result, almost all amateur telematics was more or less illegal. Naturally SIP had no way of knowing what was plugged into your telephone socket, also because what your modem lacked was generally not the electrical parameters but the sticker certifying the approval procedure. In any case, the risk of a fine or a seizure remained, especially for those who ran BBSes.
- Telecommunication prices were very high. If local calls had once been flat-rate (that is, they cost about 200 lire regardless of their length), from the 90s onward even local calls began to be charged by duration. And when I say local, I mean truly local: even calling a neighboring town could become an expensive experience, and long-distance calls made the cost skyrocket - I remember the 10,000-lire phone cards eaten up quickly in the phone booth by my friend who had a girlfriend in Legnano and called her from Varese. International calls were prohibitively expensive, on the order of a few thousand lire per minute.
- The home phone line was typically a single one; while you were connected to the BBS, nobody could call your mom or your sister. So long BBS sessions were either at night or subject to sulking in the family. 
- There was no Internet or, rather, the first providers for home as well as business use were beginning to appear at the start of the 90s. In general, an e-mail address to communicate with the whole world was well beyond the horizon for almost the entire population. In Italy there was a packet-switching network, ITAPAC, which SIP had created in the 80s, but costs and access conditions made it out of the question for mass use.

Within this gloomy picture, there were nonetheless a few bright spots:

- Computing was becoming a mass phenomenon, with assembled PCs beginning to make a fine show in almost every office and many bedrooms. I'm not talking about computers for tinkerers like the Amiga in my bedroom or the OS/2 machines of many BBSes, but about machines running Windows and typically Word and Excel, for writing letters and keeping small accounts.
- The first fast, low-cost modems were becoming available. They were no longer the US Robotics Courier HST, with their very high cost and size, but little Taiwanese boxes of varying quality and speeds of 14.4 or 19.2, which were a big step forward from the antiquated 1200 or 2400 modems, with no compression and no error correction, of the previous generation.
- In the newspapers people were beginning to talk about this mysterious "Internet" which, in the faraway Americas but also, for the savviest, in Malgesso, was taking its first steps toward a mass market.




### The Scene

#### The BBSes

In the early 90s, around Varese, there was already a sizable network of telematics experimenters who used a number of BBSes to exchange files and comments. These typically used FidoNet technology to stay connected to one another, either as proper members of the Italian FidoNet or as members of other networks that nonetheless used the same technology to exchange messages.

In Varese there were a few "historic" BBSes, and many others that came and went over time depending on how much time their sysops had and on the size of the phone bills they received.

The first BBS in Varese was certainly HAL BBS, a FidoNet node, founded by Bruno many years earlier and a reference point for all the local telematics scene. Then there was Skorpion BBS, famous at the time for its apparently endless collection of photographs of scantily clad young ladies, and also a FidoNet node. Then there was SkyLink in Malgesso, a reference point for Amiga users that later became one of the first Internet nodes in Italy, CMB in Casciago, and quite a few others that I don't remember in the Gallarate area.

A couple of times a year we would get together for the classic Fido pizza, which was a chance to meet in person after so many text messages. I have the impression that almost all the participants in the Fido pizzas went on to become IT professionals and form the original core of the Internet pioneers in Italy.


#### The Civic Network of Milan 

In parallel with the BBSes, which were rather complicated to run and also to use for anyone who wasn't a real nerd, another strand of what would come to be called civic informatics was beginning in Italy. At the computer science department of the University of Milan, RCM, the Civic Network of Milan (Rete Civica di Milano), had in fact been born, on the initiative of Professor De Cindio. The idea was to develop civic communication spaces suitable for everyone, not just engineering students, and to see how these tools would promote acquaintance among people and perhaps a democracy from below.

From a technical point of view, RCM relied on a piece of software called FirstClass, which ran on Macintosh servers and came as a graphical program working under both Windows and Macintosh. The civic network had a decidedly larger budget than any BBS of the time, probably larger than that of all the BBSes of the time put together, since it required a number of expensive Macintosh servers, a great many phone lines because the program only worked connected to the telephone, plus the not-cheap licenses for the software itself. FirstClass was designed to work above all over a local network, and only later over a modem.

#### ArcomerA

On the initiative of some students who had had the experience of the civic network of Milan, the idea was born of bringing a similar experience to Varese. The project was to be called ArcomerA, after the gate of Varese that connects the basilica with the square of the Garibaldino. 

The initiative produced many ideas about what we could do, but few feasible ones: the cost of the operation was decidedly high, and the attempts to find a line of funding from the local authorities ran up against their total incomprehension. 

On top of that, the group of organizers consisted mostly of people with no technical background, so the move from words to deeds never actually happened. 

In the course of the studies to carry out this initiative, however, I had come across a piece of software, also commercial but much cheaper than FirstClass and much more efficient, called WorldGroup. It allowed you to have a BBS with a graphical client as easy to use as that of the civic network of Milan, but with a built-in off-line reader, that is, a mode in which you could connect to the BBS for a few minutes, exchange mail and the messages you were interested in, and then disconnect to read and reply to them. 

This way the phone cost was lower for the caller and, above all, on the BBS side far fewer phone lines were needed to serve the same number of users. For those who still wanted an online experience, all the real-time chat functions and the browsing of proto-web pages were there. Besides, it wasn't sold by a company in Milan where Bruno, the one from Hal, worked. I was personally convinced that this was a good opportunity, but the group tied to the civic network of Milan did not want to consider any solution different from the Milanese one, on pain of lèse-majesté (in hindsight, one of the most successful experiences among the civic networks was the civic network of San Donato, founded by a computer science student, which relied, just like us, on WorldGroup).

#### The CSG

Another initiative, not directly related to the previous ones but joined to them by solid personal ties of friendship, was the CSG, that is, the Comitato per gli Spazi Giovanili (Committee for Youth Spaces), which had been born a few years earlier to ask for a self-managed space for the young people of Varese, identifying a valid opportunity in the building ??? that had recently been abandoned and taken over by the Municipality. 

As you can imagine, the Varese political establishment of those years had very little sympathy for what might look like a *centro sociale* (an Italian-style self-managed, often squatted, left-wing community space), and what we imagined as a space for playing music and role-playing games was imagined by the political-administrative counterpart as a sort of Varese-style Jamaica from which drunken punks would launch vandal expeditions against the elegant shops of the city center. To make a long story short, nothing ever came of it. 

The CSG did, however, have a small space at the CoopUF cooperative in via de Cristoforis, and by the end of 1995 it had somewhat run out of steam. But when people began to talk about what was happening with the civic networks, the idea of having a free space, albeit a virtual one, appealed to many of the members.


## The Birth

Forgive me, reader, but I must admit that I don't remember exactly how Pangea came about; still, in 1996 the stars had aligned and the heavens were favorable to us:

- The CSG group had a venue and a couple of million lire in the bank, with no great prospects; it was interested in funding this virtual space and in offering a venue
- With about 1 million lire we could get the WorldGroup license for five simultaneous users (the smallest one), and with the other million pay the phone costs of an ISDN line for a year or so
- Marco Ghost and Marco CJK, who dealt in hardware, could get us a small assembled server and a couple of other second-hand computers to set up the system
- Sara made a drawing of the province of Varese that would greet our visitors
- Luca of SkyLink offered us the Internet connection, a space in which to create our web site and, above all, the possibility of offering Pangea users a free e-mail address 
- A sysop (yours truly) who would run it
- Gab and CJK would advertise the initiative in the world of non-technical users

At that point, on July 15, 1996, we founded the Associazione Culturale Pangea, opened a telephone account, tried to keep expenses to a minimum, and off we went.

According to the server logs, the first users were registered on **August 6, 1996**, almost certainly during the first installation of the software. What I remember is that it was hot, and there was a certain tension after the night spent reading the ? pages of the WorldGroup manual from beginning to end. The first call to the BBS, to see whether the modem worked, was made by Iole.

The first registered users (6/8/96) were Cjk, Ghost, Lenz and Gab; on the 7th Luigi and Serena joined.


### The Venue

Pangea was on the first floor at the CoopUF, next to the projection room and a photographer's studio. There wasn't much: a couple of computers on a table (the Pangea server and an assembled Vobis with Windows 3.11), the blunzer on the floor, the ISDN socket on the wall behind us. The room was dusty, full of cigarette ash and decidedly not too clean. Then there was that time the server's CPU fan had stuck and we had fixed it with a few drops of oil asked for from the bar downstairs: at moments of peak load, a smell of French fries spread through the room. 

The room was shared with other associations that used it occasionally; we never had problems with the other users. Every now and then there was a drunk on the stairs, or Felix wanting to scrounge a cigarette, but given the essentially zero cost we certainly couldn't complain.

### The Group

Pangy's users met mainly on Pangea itself; however, by tradition, Saturday afternoon was the time when everyone who wanted to drop by and see us would meet. When there was a new user who wanted to register, or to take one of the little courses that Cristina and Michele organized, they simply dropped by on Saturday afternoon and someone was always there.

On Saturday afternoons there were also the group chats: the two phone lines on the ISDN socket let two people connect from outside at the same time, and in addition the server and the graphical client beside it let two more people on site chat all together. Chatting four at a time, in real time, in 1996 felt like a futuristic experience.

To sign up for Pangea it was enough to create an account on first connecting to the system; however, this user could do relatively few things - this was to avoid being invaded by trolls who, then as now, are never lacking. To be enabled as a full user you had to send us a copy of your ID card or come by the venue on Saturday afternoon to have your identity verified. Pangea asked for a small free donation to cover running costs and to become a member of the cultural association - about a fifth of the users had joined.

### The Association and the Funding

I don't remember exactly the roles in the association; I remember that CJK was the president and that I had no official role. If I remember rightly, the roles had been drawn by lot out of a cup at the time of the founding.

There were also non-institutional roles that everybody knew about, in particular:

- Michele took care of the graphics
- Cristina and Michele took care of training and of helping newcomers
- Luigi helped us write the newsletter and the press releases
- …

I also don't remember exactly the state of the accounts, nor whether we managed to obtain any funding over 50,000 lire from some institution or the like. What I seem to remember is that we tried to survive for as long as possible with as few resources as possible.

## How It Worked: For Users

### How to Connect

For the more advanced, or those already experienced in telematics, connecting to Pangea worked like a normal BBS. You dialed the number with the modem and, thanks to a terminal emulator, you could interact with the BBS on the other end. The BBS was fully functional in character mode: practically all the content was usable in text format as well. If I remember rightly, there was even the possibility of exchanging mail packets in QWK off-line reader mode.

The most interesting mode, though, was the one that used the graphical client. On the first connection, it was possible to download an ad-hoc client, so that you could then connect directly in graphical mode - you didn't even need to register, it was enough to log in with the user `client`. The client worked on Windows 3.1 and on the first edition of Windows 95; it did not work on Windows 98, however.

The download was quite demanding anyway: the client was about a couple of megabytes, so the full version would have taken two floppy disks. Downloading 2 MB at 14.4k takes about half an hour. The simplest option for most users was to start directly from a Pangea floppy: it was distributed free of charge at Runner Computer in Varese, or you could pick it up by dropping by our venue. This floppy contained a working, preconfigured client, so that with nothing else installed on the computer you could click on the Pangea icon and the system would connect automatically. 

On first use some components that were not on the floppy would be updated, but this would happen when you opened them - for example, the first time you opened the chats they would be very slow, after which the chat component would be available locally. The same happened with the graphics, so the more you used the system, the more responsive it became. The client was in any case able to download over several connections at once, so you could, for instance, chat while it was downloading the mail module - unlike BBSes, where during a download you couldn't do anything else.

### The First Connection 

During the first connection, the system offered you the choice of using an existing user or, if you didn't have one, creating a new one. Pangea's policy was that this was a public space, so everyone, or almost everyone, had to appear with first and last name. Only in rare cases was a nickname allowed. The user thus created was immediately operational - you could move around the system, look around, and read the discussions. If a user was created with an inappropriate or suspicious name, it would be suspended pending clarification or deleted. 

The guide (in three parts) is still available: https://web.archive.org/web/19991001201309/http://www.pangea.va.it/pangea/pc1.htm

### Becoming a User 

To become a full user you had to be verified: to do so you had to accept and sign the terms of use, which included the guiding principles, and show your ID card. The terms of use can be seen here: https://web.archive.org/web/19991118034934/http://www.pangea.va.it/pangea/pi_ru.htm


These steps could be done by mail, by printing a copy of the terms and sending it to us, or by dropping by to see us on Saturday afternoon in Varese. In exchange, you got full access and, above all, a free and immediately working e-mail address `nome.cognome@pangea.va.it`. 

At the time, to have an e-mail address you had to have an Internet subscription and often pay an extra on top of your Internet subscription - on Pangea it was completely free. This was an important draw for our users.

### The Courses 

The vast majority of Pangea's users did not come from the BBS world and often had minimal computer experience. Our goal was precisely to involve people who would not have been interested in the telematics of the time.  

To do so, however, we had to wear all the hats: from explaining what was in Pangea, to moderating the more enthusiastic users, to helping diagnose some problem with the modem or the video card driver.  The idea was that the community would take care of itself, and this generally worked quite well. People usually arrived at Pangea by word of mouth, because they knew someone who already used it and perhaps saw it at their place.

Cristina - with Michele's help - organized the Saturday courses; when there was a user (or potential user) who was interested and wanted to understand a little more, she would give a course of an hour or two to explain the basic features and how to access them. Often the course turned into a chat that ended with a beer in the late afternoon as more people arrived at the venue.

### What You Could Find in Pangea

- Mail, that is, direct messages with other users and incoming and outgoing e-mail.
- Real-time chats, especially in the evening, when someone was at the venue
- The polls of the week and their results
- Some information pages run by various associations. Each page was linked to a discussion forum. We never managed to get really important users, such as municipalities or local authorities - we had the Informagiovani, Legambiente Varese, the Gruppo Astronomico Tradatese, Agorà, ASVP and little else
- Some hypermedia information pages - the Via Verde Varesina, the Sacro Monte di Varese, and a section on first aid - all made by Michele
- The user cards, in which everyone could publish a bit of information about themselves by way of introduction (if they wanted to)
- Some file areas from which to download, for example, antivirus software or other useful computer tools, with free access. We always avoided having material that was potentially problematic in terms of content or copyright, with a view to having a virtual square open to everyone. Today you could download these directly from the Internet, but our average user had no Internet access.
- The buy/sell/swap marketplaces
- Various discussion forums on free topics - for example politics, romantic relationships, technical support on interesting subjects, chess and go, etc. a list can be found 

### The Mailing Lists

A parallel service offered by Pangea was the management of mailing lists, that is, e-mail discussion groups, for associations or interested users.

In particular, I remember a mutual-support mailing list run by a group of people with AIDS who had contacted us to create this service in a "discreet" way.


### The Web Site

Again thanks to the good offices of Saint Luca, we had access to a static web site on which to publish information about the civic network and how to connect to it. From there you could also download the famous client without having to go and pick up the floppy. Quite a few users downloaded the client at the university, copied it to a floppy, and then connected once back home.

The same space was also used by some of the associations present on Pangea to publish information for their members. A good part of this material is still available on archive.org.
